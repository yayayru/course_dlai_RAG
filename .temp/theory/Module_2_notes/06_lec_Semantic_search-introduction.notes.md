## Что делает semantic search

`semantic search` matches documents to prompts based on shared meaning and can capture nuances that keyword search misses.

Examples:

- keyword search cannot match `happy` and `glad`, even though they are synonyms;
- keyword search would incorrectly match `Python` as programming language and `Python` as snake.

Semantic search addresses these problems by working with meaning rather than exact words.

## Общая схема похожа на keyword search

At a high level, semantic search works just like keyword search.

1. Every document is mapped to a vector.
2. Prompt is also mapped to a vector.
3. Prompt vector and document vectors are compared.
4. Scores are generated.
5. Documents closest to the prompt are found.

Primary difference is how vectors are assigned.

## Embedding model

In keyword search, vector values count how often each word appears in text.

In semantic search, vectors are generated by running a document or prompt through a special mathematical model called an `embedding model`.

> `embedding model` maps text to a vector that represents a location in vector space.

Example:

- word `pizza` might be mapped to vector `(3, 1)`;
- word `bear` might be mapped to vector `(5, 2)`.

In two dimensions, these can be represented as points on the x-y axis.

## Similar meanings, nearby locations

The embedding model maps semantically similar words to nearby locations in space.

Examples:

- `food` and `cuisine` are embedded closer to one another;
- `trombone` and `cat` are embedded farther apart.

Similar meanings lead to similar positions.

## Axes are not directly interpretable

The x and y axes in the example do not have a simple interpretation.

There is no visible `food axis` or `animal axis`.

Better mental model:

- points floating around on a 2D plane;
- words with similar meaning clustered together.

Words have many complex relationships, so creating sensible clusters in two dimensions probably will not work.

## High-dimensional vector space

If vectors had three components, they could be embedded into three-dimensional space, giving more room for clusters of related concepts and nuanced relationships.

Most embedding models use vectors with hundreds or thousands of components.

This gives much more flexibility in where each point can be embedded.

It is impossible to graph or imagine this high-dimensional space, but mathematically the same principles hold:

- vectors give coordinates of locations in that space;
- similar concepts embedded close together;
- dissimilar concepts embedded farther apart.

## Inputs beyond individual words

Embedding models exist for many kinds of input data:

- individual words;
- sentences;
- entire documents.

Different models take different inputs, but in each case output a single vector that specifies a point in space.

If vectors are closer together, the pieces of text have similar meaning.

Example sentences:

1. `He spoke softly in class.`
2. `He whispered quietly during class.`
3. `Her daughter brightened the gloomy day.`

When projected into vector space:

- vectors for the first two sentences are closer together;
- third sentence is far away from the other two.

## Measuring distance between vectors

To quantify similarity of different pieces of text, measure the distance between their vectors.

### Euclidean distance

`Euclidean distance` measures how far apart two vectors are by drawing a straight line from one vector to the other.

It is the shortest possible distance between them.

Formula is essentially the Pythagorean theorem scaled up across more dimensions.

Limitation:

- in very high-dimensional space, every point tends to be pretty far away from every other point.

### Cosine similarity

`cosine similarity` is more commonly used.

It measures similarity in the direction of two vectors, regardless of whether they are close in space.

Example:

- vector `(10, 10)` and `(100, 100)` are not close to one another;
- but they face in the same direction.

Cosine similarities range:

- `1`: vectors face exactly the same direction;
- `-1`: vectors face exactly the opposite direction.

### Dot product

`dot product` measures the length of the projection of one vector onto another.

Behavior:

- if two vectors are similar in length and direction, projection is larger;
- if they are at a 90 degree angle, projection length is zero;
- if they face opposite directions, dot product is negative.

For both dot product and cosine similarity:

- higher values reflect closer vectors;
- closer vectors reflect more similar concepts.

Cosine similarity ranges from `-1` to `1`.

Dot product can take any value between negative and positive infinity.

## Semantic search process

Semantic search uses distance measure as follows:

1. All documents are projected into vector space by the embedding model.
2. Documents with similar meaning are closer together.
3. Documents with dissimilar meaning are farther apart.
4. Prompt is embedded to get its own vector.
5. Distance between prompt vector and each document vector is measured.
6. Closest documents have the most similar meaning.
7. Documents are sorted by distance from the prompt.
8. Documents with the shortest distance are returned.

> Важный процесс: semantic search ranks documents by comparing prompt embedding with document embeddings in vector space.

## Вывод

Semantic search is fairly straightforward if one takes for granted that embedding model works properly.

The key assumption:

- similar concepts are mapped to nearby locations;
- relevance can be quantified by distance between vectors.

Understanding how embedding models are trained can deepen understanding of semantic search, but this lecture focuses on the high-level mechanism.
